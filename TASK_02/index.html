1. What is phishing?

Phishing is a cyber attack where criminals pretend to be a trusted person or organisation (a bank, your employer, a delivery company, a government office) to trick you into revealing passwords, OTPs, card details, or into installing malware.

It works because it targets people, not technology. Attackers create urgency, fear or greed so that you act before you think.

Why it matters: phishing is consistently one of the most common ways breaches begin – a single click or a single shared OTP can expose an account, a company network, or your savings.

2. Common types of phishing

EMAIL

Bulk phishing

Mass emails pretending to be a bank, courier or online store, asking you to "verify" or "update" an account.
TARGETED

Spear phishing

Personalised for you, using your name, job or colleagues found on social media or LinkedIn.
EXECUTIVES

Whaling / BEC

Impersonates a CEO or vendor to request urgent payments or sensitive data (Business Email Compromise).
SMS

Smishing

Text messages with fake KYC, parcel, prize or electricity-bill warnings and a short link.
VOICE

Vishing

Phone calls from "bank officials", "tech support" or "police" pressing you to share OTPs or install remote-access apps.
QR

Quishing

Malicious QR codes on stickers, posters or emails that lead to fake login or payment pages.

3. How to identify phishing

Suspicious emails

  • Sender mismatch: display name says "Your Bank" but the address is support@yourbank-secure.example, a lookalike domain.
  • Urgent or threatening tone: "Account suspended in 24 hours", "Immediate action required".
  • Generic greeting: "Dear customer" instead of your name.
  • Unexpected attachments (.zip, .html, .exe, macro-enabled Office files) or invoices you never requested.
  • Spelling, grammar and branding errors, though modern phishing can be polished, so don't rely on this alone.
  • Links that don't match their text: hover (or long-press on mobile) to see the real destination.

Fake login pages and fraudulent websites

5. Social engineering techniques

Phishing is a form of social engineering: manipulating people psychologically. Common levers:

TechniqueHow it worksExample
Urgency / scarcityLimited time pushes quick action"Pay within 2 hours or electricity will be cut."
AuthorityPretends to be boss, police, bank, government"This is the CEO, I need gift cards now."
FearThreatens loss, arrest or account closure"Your SIM will be blocked."
Greed / rewardPrizes, refunds, easy money"You've won a lottery / cashback."
Trust & familiarityUses real names or hijacked threadsA reply inside a genuine email chain from a hacked colleague.
PretextingInvents a believable story to get infoCaller pretends to be IT support doing "a security check".

TailgatingPhysically follows you into a restricted area"Can you hold the door? I forgot my badge."

6. Real-life case studies

Case 1 – Fake invoices cost two tech giants over $100 million (2013–2015)

A fraudster impersonated a genuine Asian hardware supplier and sent convincing invoices, contracts and letters to staff at Google and Facebook, who paid the money into accounts he controlled. He was later arrested and pleaded guilty; most funds were recovered.

Lesson: verify payment-detail changes through a second, known channel (call a number you already have).

Case 2 – Fake "security alert" and the 2016 US election hack

Staff at a political campaign received an email that looked like a Google security alert urging a password change. Clicking the link led to a fake page that captured credentials, leading to large-scale email leaks.

Lesson: never use links in alerts – go to the service directly. Turn on multi-factor authentication.

Case 3 – 2020 Twitter breach via phone phishing

Attackers phoned Twitter employees pretending to be internal IT, got them to enter credentials on a fake VPN page, and used the access to take over high-profile accounts for a cryptocurrency scam.

Lesson: vishing works on tech-savvy staff too. Verify callers independently; use phishing-resistant MFA (security keys).

Case 4 – The everyday "KYC update" SMS

A very common scam in India and elsewhere: an SMS claims your bank account or wallet will be frozen unless you "complete KYC" through a link or by calling a number. The page or caller then collects card details and OTPs, or asks you to install a remote-control app.

Lesson: banks never ask for OTP/PIN. Use the official app or branch, and report fraud quickly (in India: call 1930 or visit cybercrime.gov.in).

7. Practical security tips

Stop – Look – Verify

Pause on any urgent message. Check sender, link and request. Verify using an official app, website or phone number you already know.

Use MFA

Enable multi-factor authentication everywhere. Prefer authenticator apps or security keys over SMS codes.

Password manager

Unique passwords per site. A manager won't autofill on a lookalike domain, which is a built-in warning.

Never share OTPs

OTPs, PINs and CVVs are for you only. No genuine employee will ask for them.

Keep software updated

Update OS, browser and antivirus; enable spam filtering.

Be careful with attachments & QR codes

Don't open unexpected files. Preview the URL after scanning a QR before you open it.

If you clicked or shared something

  1. Disconnect the device from the network if you opened a file.
  2. Change the affected passwords immediately (from a clean device) and enable MFA.
  3. Contact your bank or provider to freeze cards or accounts.
  4. Report to your IT/security team, the impersonated company, and the authorities (in India: cybercrime.gov.in or helpline 1930).
  5. Monitor accounts for unusual activity.
